Why can’t anyone copy\n> Google’s technical choices without first understanding the culture?*\n>\n> Let me answer that with an example from the book\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/dde0946e-109e-48f2-93bd-eea6efe6df6a?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> and my talk\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/a39a0df2-aa2b-4728-845a-e28bb61e136d?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>.\n> Google has a process called Large-Scale Changes that allows any engineer at\n> the company to propose an edit to the entire codebase and, if approved,\n> execute it themselves.\n>\n> Typical LSCs range into the millions of lines of code. I’m actually\n> working on an LSC to refactor the format of TODOs across our entire\n> codebase, in every language, to validate some new techniques. If we are\n> successful, we will change millions of lines of code across parts of the\n> codebase to which I have absolutely no connection. I probably won’t even\n> look at the code. I will rely on the reviewing engineers to sign off on the\n> change. Keep in mind this has all been done without AI for the last 15\n> years.\n>\n> Consider what is culturally necessary for this process to work:\n>\n> -\n>\n> I need to be able to access all the code, whether I work on it or not.\n> -\n>\n> I need a way to validate my changes don’t break anything - which means\n> everyone has to have bought into testing, and I need to be able to reliably\n> run those tests.\n> -\n>\n> I need the belief that such work is valuable and that reviewers need\n> to spend time reviewing my changes.\n> -\n>\n> I need the foundational belief that maintenance of our codebase is a\n> shared responsibility.\n> -\n>\n> Specific to my LSC on TODOs, there needs to be a shared cultural\n> belief in standardization.\n>\n> You can build incredibly powerful code mutation tools, but without the\n> right engineering culture, the ability to navigate the human aspects of the\n> codebase will doom you to failure. As long as software involves people,\n> culture, processes, and tools, they are all dependent on each other.\n>\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/a39a0df2-aa2b-4728-845a-e28bb61e136d?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>*4.\n> In your talk, you use Conway’s Law as the door into everything else. Now\n> that agents write and change code, do they become part of that shape too?*\n>\n> Conway was one of the first to observe the deep connection between how we\n> organize and the systems we produce. That connection often seems invisible\n> because, as engineers, we are not really expected to think about how our\n> organizations work, and we can often get away without worrying too much. AI\n> may change that dynamic quite a bit.\n>\n> AI's ability to amplify outcomes will make many things that were\n> previously invisible much more visible. For example, in a world with\n> reliable agentic programming, your organization's decision-making abilities\n> will be under intense pressure to accelerate to keep pace with software\n> construction. How much did the average engineer even think about their\n> executive’s decision-making process before?\n>\n> One of the interesting things about agents is that they aren’t impacted by\n> time zones; they don’t require physical proximity to build trust bonds, and\n> as far as I can tell, they don’t have a notion of politics. These are some\n> of the underlying forces that give rise to Conway’s law\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/31f2845b-4ecc-4741-bc62-fde511b208c5?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>.\n> That suggests Conway’s law could be a byproduct of the limitation of human\n> cooperation skills and agents won’t necessarily be affected. Agents will\n> almost certainly have subtle organizing principles, likely influenced by\n> whatever incentives they recognize, but we shouldn’t expect those\n> principles to reflect our human experience.\n> [image: Conway's Law illustration]\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/31f2845b-4ecc-4741-bc62-fde511b208c5?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4> Conway’s\n> Law from Laws of Software Engineering\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/31f2845b-4ecc-4741-bc62-fde511b208c5?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> *5. One of the most interesting things you said is that all your internal\n> APIs just became public, because an agent won’t respect a boundary you only\n> ever enforced with good manners. Where should a team start hardening?*\n>\n> Here is the simple formula I’m using:\n>\n> 1.\n>\n> Build an inventory of all internal APIs (intentionally exposed or not)\n> and all data stores. You have to know where everything is. Remember that\n> agents can find everything and are persistent.\n> 2.\n>\n> Develop a risk formula that captures what your organization considers\n> most important. Use it to prioritize hardening your most critical assets.\n> 3.\n>\n> Use Platform Engineering techniques to create well-lit paths for\n> agents and humans alike and lock everything else down. If you lack the\n> capability to lock things down, you will need to build that first. You can\n> start by preventing agents from taking any destructive actions, especially\n> in your production systems.\n> 4.\n>\n> Develop really robust internal observability. You want to know how\n> APIs and data are being accessed at all times.\n>\n> 6. If no one is writing the code, a year from now, who is actually\n> watching the codebase as it grows?\n>\n> If we aren’t careful, the answer is no one, and that’s a huge problem. The\n> average software system is too complex for any one person to reliably\n> reason about today. Luckily, it is still within reach of human-scale teams\n> to evaluate. If we grow those systems by 10x in size without improving our\n> ability to reason about them, we will completely lose the ability to make\n> changes safely, which could severely limit our ability to evolve and\n> maintain systems.\n>\n> From the earliest days of LLM-driven development, I have tried to steer\n> folks to spend as much time building tools for understanding as we do for\n> creation. Alas, I haven’t been as convincing as I would like.\n> *7. You wrote the testing chapters in the book, and yet on stage you\n> admitted you don’t have the integration testing tools you want. When output\n> increases 10x, which part of testing breaks first?*\n>\n> To clarify quickly, I was the editor of the series of testing chapters and\n> wrote the Testing Overview\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/dde0946e-109e-48f2-93bd-eea6efe6df6a?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>.\n>\n>\n> I don’t have the integration tools I want, but it is not for lack of\n> trying. Google had good-sized teams of incredibly smart people building\n> better integration test tooling, yet we still have a long way to go. I\n> think the problem stems from the fact that unless teams consider\n> integration testing as an essential system property to be evolved in\n> lockstep with their systems - equal to concurrency, or reliability in\n> importance - the complexity of the underlying system rapidly escapes our\n> ability to build system-level tests. *Truly excellent integration testing\n> will come from a system explicitly designed to be integration testable*,\n> however most teams don’t have the luxury of investing in that kind of work,\n> maybe AI can make it more cost effective?\n>\n> As far as what breaks first? I think teams will likely drown in unit tests\n> at first because agents are really good at writing them. This is a problem\n> we have wrestled with at Google even before AI. It is really easy to\n> generate lots of little tests that appear to add value but are low-value\n> because they duplicate other tests, lack precise assertions, are flaky, are\n> tedious to maintain, or otherwise fall short. Also, keep in mind that today it\n> is very common to require* all tests to pass* before shipping; the more\n> tests you have, the harder it is for all of them to pass at any one time,\n> even if they are independently very reliable.\n>\n> *One thing I have learned after nearly 14 years of Google is that it is\n> possible to have too much of a good thing, even tests!* This isn’t an\n> invitation to give up testing, far from it; this is a call to apply a much\n> more intentional approach and invest in reimagining how we validate\n> software when code is cheap.\n> [image: Visit Googleplex: A Comprehensive Tour Of Google's Headquarters]\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/f1a580c0-3b1c-404e-ba58-34e81922d9a6?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4> Google\n> building\n> *8. You said the thing keeping you up at night is intellectual control,\n> whether a human can still reason about the system in front of them. Is AI\n> making it worse?*\n>\n> At the moment, AI is only making this problem worse because most of the\n> energy invested has been focused on code creation. As I mentioned\n> previously, this kinda makes sense because validating generated code is\n> much more straightforward than validating nuanced guidance about system\n> health.\n>\n> Mechanisms to hill-climb towards correct code are well understood and\n> apparently pretty cheap to implement. Quicksort is quicksort, and it’s easy\n> to test. Diagnosing novel errors in a production system requires the\n> ability to put the complete system state into a context window and pair\n> that with guidance on the nature of a highly bespoke system with no analog\n> anywhere in the world. That’s still an unsolved problem as far as I can\n> tell.\n>\n> I do think AI has the potential to be massively helpful here because it is\n> incredibly good at identifying patterns and predicting outcomes. What we\n> are missing is a massive context-engineering exercise to make our systems\n> more legible and predictable for AI.\n> *9. How do you teach someone ten years of judgment in six months, when\n> they have fifty agents and none of the instinct yet? Have you seen anything\n> that even partly works?*\n>\n> Honest answer… I have no idea. I have been teaching new technical leads\n> (TLs) at Google since 2017, and one thing remains constant: it is very hard\n> to learn judgment and develop intuition just by listening to lectures or\n> participating in discussion groups. Real intuition comes from making a\n> judgment, taking action, and experiencing the consequences. That’s the loop\n> we need to put new developers through more often.\n>\n> One idea I have been experimenting with is using Role-Playing Games to\n> help build the kind of discernment needed. I believe the SRE community at\n> Google has seen good results with a form of RPG called Wheel of Misfortune\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/4f5cc302-b3f3-4bb4-824b-e1867238013e?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>,\n> in which participants simulate incidents and are challenged to address them.\n>\n> I've been talking with my friend Titus\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/9c485245-0c0f-4f2e-8bb1-5fa1357c0ae0?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> quite a bit about this problem, and he thinks investing in apprenticeship\n> techniques like pair programming is a great way to quickly transmit\n> high-bandwidth experience to new developers. I think he's right. I think\n> deeper human connection is a great way to accelerate the transfer of\n> engineering experience.\n>\n> The other thing I’d recommend is that *every single incoming software\n> engineer learn something about system theory and systems thinking*.\n> Learning about general system dynamics and system patterns can help you\n> develop analytical tools and mental models for the complexity we are\n> working with, and, specifically, it can help you gain clarity at an\n> abstraction level above the individual lines of code that agents are\n> increasingly responsible for. It isn’t exactly the same thing as deep\n> intuition, but it can be powerful enough to achieve similar outcomes.\n>\n> Milan here: *check these two books I recommend\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/28fd8d7b-c727-4d25-9e9c-925861e976c6?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> on improving systems thinking*.\n>\n>\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/87cae5ca-2769-40cb-b3ac-3ffb5b07c92b?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> *10. You ended by telling the room they have more agency than they think.\n> For an engineer who cares about quality and feels carried along by all of\n> this, where do they actually start on Monday morning?*\n>\n> The first thing you can do is define what quality actually means for your\n> system and your business. Figure out the indicators of both technical and\n> business impact that would give a leading indication that quality is\n> suffering. This is not going to be an easy exercise, and very likely the\n> things you think matter don’t matter as much to your business leads as they\n> probably should. However, starting the conversation about what matters will\n> make it much easier to address impacts as they emerge.\n>\n> If you were to do two things on Monday, the second would be to try to map\n> out your developer ecosystem - all the parts, cultural and technical - and\n> try to work out the second- and third-order consequences of *much more\n> code*.\n>\n> Be creative and don’t forget the hidden parts of the ecosystem you have\n> taken for granted for years. Everything is up for grabs if we achieve a 10x\n> increase in programming productivity.\n> ------------------------------\n> *📔 The Laws of Software Engineering book\n> <https://substack.com/redirect/5083520e-a013-429a-80c0-c1a709a0af47?j=eyJ1IjoidGNzdjcifQ.Hy0mZXEUTYJbkh50bMrIPGMLtR32SOZ1MMSTOaxglK4>\n> is out*\n>\n> The book began as a document I wrote over the years.
