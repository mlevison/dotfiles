## AI Tsunami
I can't go even a few minutes without hearing more noise about AI. I've survived every Gartner Hype Cycle since they were established, and this is noisest of them all. Between 1995 and 2001, the dot-com bubble was based on the promise that the internet would change everything. It did, just a lot later than Nortel, JDS Uniphase, Pets.com and WebVan expected it to pay off.

AI is a louder, noisier version of the dot-com bubble. Real changes are happening, and they will impact our world. We can't predict whether this is a bubble or the beginning of a new era. If it's a bubble, we will still end up with GenAI tools with capabilities similar to those we have today. If it’s not a bubble and companies like OpenAI and Anthropic succeed, then the size of the changes will be much larger.

No matter what happens, we need to adapt to the changes. The key question is, are these tools that augment our work or are corporations going to shrink?

Even if the tools don’t get better, they're already impacting our world. In software development, we already see more lines of code being written and more bugs being introduced: https://agilepainrelief.com/blog/ai-generated-code-quality-problems/. Yet, I also find teams that claim to be using AI and knocking it out of the park. Better quality than ever before.

As it currently stands, AI appears to be an amplifier. If you’re already doing well, AI seems to be a force multiplier. If your team/organization has challenges, AI will make them worse. Classic example: A team with either a bottleneck in either Decision-Making or Quality Assurance. They use AI to generate code, and now the bottleneck is worse. There are more decisions to make and a larger product surface area to test.

## Agile More Relevant Than Ever
Agile has always been about adapting to change. GenAI requires us to: look for the bottlenecks; run more process experiments; and run more product experiments. From a product management perspective, I can already see that these tools make it easier to run more product experiments. In my own experience, they make it easier to move my data from one product to another. Without good Quality and Product Market Fit, speed doesn’t matter.

There is a lot of work ahead of us to explore this new territory.

## Intentional
I travelled to the Canary Islands for a hiking vacation. On the Island of La Gomera, I experienced [Storm "Therese](https://lagomera1.blogspot.com/2026/03/la-gomera-hit-by-storms-torrential-rain.html)". Hiking got replaced by reflection and reading more of [Chris Bailey's book: "Intentional: How to Finish What You Start"](https://www.amazon.ca/Intentional-How-Finish-What-Start/dp/1039011608/ref=sr_1_1?crid=3PAS3E9MMIJSK&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.6bbqAWns9DH4qy97NTKbr0uaVrrK7wWxnu7IEn2rRxI.foN2UrwRvkBVjvzc2RS1-HNZY6Fg_sASA-Svcnn5i08&dib_tag=se&keywords=intentional+chris+bailey&qid=1775144954&sprefix=chris+bai%2Caps%2C210&sr=8-1). I thought I was reading this only for me. Now, as I look back, I realize the book has value for Agile teams. By default, as humans we spend about 5% of our time planning and 95% implementing those plans. (I don't even think I hit 5%.) The research suggests that the number should be closer to 20% planning and 80% action. In the book, he shows that goal attainment happens in part because we plan on many levels, from life goals, all the way down to what is the best thing I should do today.

Thinking about Scrum, all of Sprint Planning, Review, Retrospective, and Daily Scrum are about planning and goal attainment. Officially, they're supposed to occupy 10% of the Sprint. We also have Backlog Refinement, which, based on experience, takes up another 3-5% of a Sprint. Scrum anticipated the research that Chris found for his book by more than a decade.

When we see teams first adopt Scrum, there is often pushback: too much time spent on planning and not enough time doing. I've always known the pushback was misplaced and now I have research to support it.

If you want to make Backlog Refinement better for your team, look at: [Product Backlog Refinement Hell: 3 Problems and Solutions](https://agilepainrelief.com/blog/product-backlog-refinement-hell-solutions/)

As we move into a world where GenAI can help us do some of the heavy lifting, intentions and planning will be ever more important. Shipping unproven features faster is not winning. Yet many teams are using GenAI to rip through their Product Backlog without considering whether the work has any value.

To thrive in this new world, we will need better Product Ownership and Quality.

My topic: How is GenAI reshaping Product Ownership?

AI Vendors would have us believe that developing with their tools is easy and that code is almost free. In reality, we all know that is far from true. In this environment, we can't compete on the number of features. We compete on Product Solution Fit and Quality. We get there using tools that the Agile community has been honing for years:
- Lean Startup
- Lean UX
- Set-Based Design
- BDD (Behaviour Driven Development)

Eventually, this all leads to: "Continuous Discovery." The idea is that instead of working from a large Product Backlog, we work with the client on a continuous basis. We discover the next most valuable work and then build it.

In a world that is being upended on a regular basis, having a roadmap that extends 6-12 months out is waste (in the Lean Waste sense). Keep using Story Maps or other Strategic tools to show options, but organizations need to acknowledge that our map is showing options and choices.

### What's good for one person may not be good for a team
Assuming I'm working on the highest-priority things at any given moment, I am the bottleneck. Speeding up that activity, assuming it's not thinking/reflective work, is a good thing. (This also assumes that I can trust that the output is correct. Again, in my narrow context, this is true.)

When we're part of a team, making an individual more productive in the wrong place will just make the bottleneck worse. The now-classic example of generating a large new feature for your product just makes the review and testing process worse. In almost all teams I see, the review process was already the bottleneck. (Hence the strong recommendation not to use Pull Requests and instead do either Pair or Ensemble Programming.)

These problems are made worse by perverse incentives. In some organizations, people have AI usage incorporated into their performance review. What could possibly go wrong? Surprise! One team member generates multiple new features with 1000+ line pull requests, then drowns the rest of the team in a review overload. Measuring individual productivity has always been a bad idea. Measuring in an organization that is using AI is even worse.

### Measuring the wrong thing is expensive
If GenAI is an amplifier, then it amplifies both the good and the bad. If your system is measuring velocity or individual productivity, it will be amplified. If your system measures AI usage, people will find a way to use it. You will soon get a large bill from Anthropic/OpenAI to celebrate your success. And you’ll be swimming in a sea of AI-generated technical debt and security flaws.

Focus on the product and the team, not the individual. Don’t measure AI usage or velocity.

======== 2026-06-18-Failure-Bow-I-Sent-Two-Copies-of-the-Same-Newsletter-Last-Week.md ========
[Failure Bow](https://agilepainrelief.com/glossary/failure-bow/), I sent everyone two copies of last week’s newsletter. I didn’t even need a generative AI to help me make this mistake.

I'm trying to automate all of the repetitive tasks I do to run Agile Pain Relief. Scheduling and sending the newsletter are among them. Over a month ago, I automated that process, and it has been working well. I write in Obsidian/Markdown and then use the Cakemail API to send it to everyone. I never need to open the web interface and fiddle with formatting.

This past week, I tried using the A/B subject line test feature in Cakemail to see which subject line would get more people to open the newsletter.

Instead, I sent two copies of the same newsletter to everyone. The irony is that I try to avoid using web interfaces to do things like this because I'm more likely to make mistakes, and here I did it anyway.

The real problem here is that I was trying to go too fast and didn’t take the time to log in to the Cakemail web interface to see what it would send.

Automation is excellent. However, it really does pay to check the output carefully the first few times before trusting it.

## GenAI - the Costs Are Going Up
The whole GenAI industry is about to suffer a massive cost increase, which will force us all to question how much value it adds. It feels like GenAI has been sold to CEOs as a magic bullet that will replace many of their employees. We've already seen the evidence that this is a gross exaggeration. Useful tools, yes. Replacement for people, no. If anything, they need more human oversight and not less.

Today I'm focusing on the economics. We've been promised that GenAI was different from every other technology ever. As OpenAI and Anthropic approach IPOs, we’re starting to learn more about the reality. These companies are entirely normal venture-backed startups; they attempt to hook you with a lower price and then raise it to make a profit. Unlike most venture-backed startups, it looks like prices will need to increase by 10-15x to make a profit.

The CEOs of these companies sometimes claim, “We’re losing money on training, but breaking even on inference.” The evidence doesn’t even seem to support that.

The most interesting read of the week on this topic was from Gerben Wierda: "Anthropic/OpenAI may be spending more than $1000 for every $100 you pay them". It's a dense read; the key takeaway is that the models are getting better by using more tokens. This is not a good thing. The more tokens they use, the more expensive it will be for us when they start charging honest prices.

This is already happening; Anthropic's initial release of the Fable 5.0 model will soon switch to API-based pricing. (Assuming the US government allows them to release it at all.)

Just to make it worse, when we're using these models on a large codebase (or any other large dataset), token usage increases. More for the LLM to read == more tokens. This is especially hard because we can never predict at the start of a session with a GenAI how many tokens it will use. When tokens become real money, this will be expensive fast. It might be cheap to generate the core of an application, but increasingly expensive to build out the rest. Let alone maintain it.

The question that should be on everyone’s mind: Can we afford the code we've already written with GenAI?

If "AI" is a feature in your product, can you afford to pay for it?
### The answer to the cost crunch is hiding in plain sight: Modern Agile Practices are required
When you're building applications, focus first and foremost on making sure that the features we're planning to build have been validated with customers. Instead of trying to build everything in the Product Backlog, focus on validating the next few features with customers. Lean Startup and/or Lean UX experiments are a good approach here. Use GenAI here to build prototypes and get feedback - amazing.

Slice the work into small parts, then collaborate to create the Acceptance Criteria. This is [Product Backlog Refinement](https://agilepainrelief.com/blog/scrum-product-backlog-refinement/) and [Example Mapping](https://agilepainrelief.com/blog/example-mapping-your-secret-weapon-for-effective-acceptance-criteria/) in practice. The GenAI can be used as an aid to ask questions when we're stuck and test for missed edge cases or Acceptance Criteria. It doesn’t replace the people doing the work.

If it's going to work on the codebase, give it a narrow scope. For example, write acceptance tests for a specific feature based on the acceptance criteria. Then perhaps work on it iteratively to create the feature itself.

If this looks a lot like traditional Scrum and Agile Engineering Practices, that’s because it appears to be the only sustainable way to use GenAI when you actually have to pay the real cost of the tokens.

If we're going to thrive in this new world, we need to double down on many of the existing Agile practices to improve product-market fit, quality and flow.

This is the heart of what I teach in [Surviving the AI Tsunami](https://agilepainrelief.com/surviving-the-ai-tsunami-an-evidenced-based-course/) - using the Agile practices you already have to turn GenAI into validated value instead of a runaway bill.

## Backlog Refinement Anti-Patterns
I published a blog post: "[How Many of These Backlog Refinement Anti-Patterns Does Your Team Have?](https://agilepainrelief.com/blog/how-many-of-these-backlog-refinement-anti-patterns-does-your-team-have/)" and even here an LLM managed to make an interesting mistake.

*Background:* I do my writing in Markdown (Obsidian) and the website is built with Astro (a static site generator that uses MDX - Markdown with JSX).

When a blog post is ready for publishing, I copy the Markdown file into the Astro project. Since there are a number of small formatting differences between Obsidian and Astro (handling of images, callouts, etc.), I have a Claude Skill that is supposed to do the conversion. Claude deleted the file. It had found a rule to check for warnings, found warnings associated with the post and deleted it.

Since it was just operating on a copy of the original Markdown file this didn’t matter.
